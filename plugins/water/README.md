# water

A water surface over the HTML drawn in a canvas. A tap drops a pebble into the pond: a few broad rings that the wave equation carries outwards for about 2 s, in the same water as the wake a moving pointer leaves. The page below is the floor of the pool: it is refracted through the surface normals and lit by caustics computed as light is (a refracted mesh of the surface, energy conserving), and the surface reflects a sky, a window light and a sun. Clicks and typing are remapped through the displacement to the field the user sees. At rest it costs nothing: no effect frame, no animation loop.

An **example** effect plugin of this library, not part of the polyfill: nothing in `src/` imports it, and the polyfill, the frame cap and the effects host work without it. The effects host and its plugins are extensions of this library, not part of the HTML-in-Canvas proposal; native implementations do not provide them ([details](../../docs/guides/EFFECTS-PLUGINS.md#extensions-not-the-standard)). Like the host, it is **experimental** ([STABILITY.md](../../docs/STABILITY.md)): its options, actions and look may change in any minor release.

## What it does

- **Drops.** A tap is a pebble: four impulses on the wake field, 90 ms apart, at the tap: a smooth crater of radius 22 CSS px, 12 CSS px deep (at a tap's strength), the water that rushes back into it (a rebound in the middle, the 'plop'), and two smaller swings after. The wave equation carries each impulse outwards as a ring, so a tap rings out as a few separated rings in the wakes' language, with their caustics, their walls and their cost (independent of the number of taps). The rain of the demo falls on the pond as raindrops: 0.65 of a pebble in size and at most 0.6 of its depth. The steepest slope at the impact is about 40 degrees.
- **The wakes.** A grid over the canvas, of cells of about 3 CSS px (at most 65,536 cells), runs the damped wave equation at a fixed 120 steps per second, whatever the display rate and the frame cap (the first frame after the surface wakes sets the phase of the steps half a step away from the frame times, so a 120 Hz display gets one step in every frame, its first one included, also when the frame times jitter): an isotropic 9-point Laplacian, a viscosity that damps short ripples faster than long ones, and walls at the edges of the canvas that reflect the ripples. It runs on the CPU, so that the input mapper reads the very field the GPU draws (see [Input](#input)), and only while it holds a wake. Drops and wakes add up, as linear waves do.
- **The picture.** Per device pixel, the shader evaluates the field as a cubic B-spline (smooth normals and curvature, no grid pattern) and draws:
  - refraction: the page is sampled where the refracted view ray meets it (40 CSS px of displacement per unit of slope at intensity 1: text and field borders bend under a crest);
  - caustics, computed as light is (the method of Evan Wallace's WebGL Water): a mesh of the water surface, a vertex every 2 CSS px over the canvas and a margin, refracts light that falls from straight above by Snell's law (n = 1.333) onto the page, which lies 320 CSS px below for the light, so that a drop's caustic rings are centred on the drop (light from aside would shift them by about a fifth of the depth: seen from above, a wave whose light lands beside it); each triangle adds the ratio of its area on the surface to its area on the page into an r16float texture of one texel per CSS px (4x multisampled, so that a focused triangle thinner than a texel still lands its light; the texture and its multisampled target, 7,040,000 bytes at 1100 x 640 CSS px, are held only while caustics can show: not with `light: 0`, `intensity: 0` or reduced motion, made at once when an option turns them on, and released 2 s after an option turns them off, so that a slider dragged through 0 does not free and make them at every crossing). The light is conserved: it gathers into sharp lines under the ripples and leaves the troughs darker, its mean stays 1 (0.998 in the GPU test), and still water lights the page uniformly, with no pass at all. The page is lit 85 % by this light and 15 % by the sky (applied to its linear colour), and the water scatters a little of the focused light along its brightest lines, so that they show over dark content too;
  - Fresnel reflection (Schlick, F0 = 0.02 for water) of a sky, a soft window light and a small sun, seen from a viewer above the middle of the canvas: still water mirrors the window faintly over its top left, ripples break the reflection up and make the sun glint. The light the water adds (the reflection and the caustic web) is compressed with a soft knee: at most 38 % of the range per channel, so it never hides what lies below;
  - absorption: a little more blue-green where the water is deeper, in the troughs;
  - a dither of half a level against banding.
- **Input.** A pointerdown (a click, a tap) drops a pebble there, exactly at the event's point (a test holds the caustic rings within 1 CSS px of it); one event is one drop, also where it reaches both the canvas and the live layer. A moving pointer leaves a wake along its path, deeper when it moves faster, and deeper for a drag (a pressed button, a finger) than for a hovering mouse.
- **At rest.** Once the surface is flat again, below what a pixel shows (no pixel moved by more than 0.1 CSS px, no caustic focus changed by more than 1.2 %, at intensity 1; proportionally more at a lower intensity), the field is cleared, the drops are forgotten and the instance stops being `continuous`: the host renders nothing more until the page or the water changes. A pebble is still again about 3 s later at the default intensity, the rain of the action `demo` about 2 s after its last drop, a long stir of the whole canvas about 4 s after it ends.

### Why the drops are made this way

A tap and a wake are the same water: both are impulses on one height field that the wave equation advances, so a tap has the wakes' waves, caustics and walls, and the cost of a frame does not grow with the number of taps. A pebble is not one impulse but four, 90 ms apart (the crater, the water that rushes back into it, two smaller swings), so that it rings out as a few separated rings, as a stone in a pond does, rather than as one ring that travels unchanged. The wave equation has no dispersion of its own (dispersive height fields exist: iWave, Tessendorf 2004; spectral and wave-packet methods); here the train of impulses gives the rings, and the viscosity of the grid damps the short ripples first.

**Extended range.** On a host with `hdr: true` ([guide](../../docs/guides/EFFECTS-PLUGINS.md#121-extended-range-contexthdr)) the page under the water, the sky and the window it mirrors and the light it scatters stay under white, as on a standard target (which clamps the whole picture). Two lights go above it: the sun's disc mirrored by the slope of a ripple (a glint a few pixels wide, only where the surface slopes), and the page under the brightest caustic lines, where the focused sunlight (from 4 times the light of still water, in full from 10 times) makes a bright page brighter than white; a dark page under the same line stays under white. Measured in the plugin gallery (canvas 1100 x 640 at device pixel ratio 1, headless Chrome, a probe stage after the plugin; the demo, a pointer sweep and a click): still water: nothing above white; on the form scene at most 271 px above white in a frame (0.04 % of the canvas, 25 of them above 1.4), largest value 1.85; on the daylight scene at most 360 px above white in a frame (0.05 % of the canvas, 128 of them above 1.4), largest value 1.76. Before the plugin told the page from the light, the same run gave nothing on the form and nothing on the daylight scene. On a standard target the picture is the one it always was, bit for bit.

## Usage

```html
<script src="dist/hic-live-polyfill.iife.min.js"></script>
<script src="dist/hic-live-effects.iife.min.js"></script>
<script src="dist/plugins/water.iife.min.js"></script>   <!-- window.HicLivePlugins.water -->
<script type="module">
  const host = await HTMLInCanvasEffects.createHost(canvas, form, { interposer: true });
  const water = await host.addPlugin(HicLivePlugins.water.plugin, { intensity: 0.5 });
  water.mountUI(document.getElementById('controls'));                    // the plugin's intensity slider
  await water.trigger('demo');                                          // a short rain; 'calm' once the surface is still
  await water.trigger('drop', { point: { x: 120, y: 80 }, strength: 1.5 });
</script>
```

ES modules: `import { plugin } from '@giuseppe-scotto-lavina/html-in-canvas-polyfill/plugins/water'`. [`examples/effect-plugin/`](../../examples/effect-plugin/) uses it in a complete page.

## Options

| option | default | |
|---|---|---|
| `intensity` | 0.5 | 0 to 1: the strength of the water (its refraction and its light); 0 shows the source unchanged |
| `light` | 1 | 0 to 1: the caustics, the reflection and the glints; 0 keeps the refraction only |
| `ambient` | 0 | 0 to 1: a gentle swell of three long waves that never settles. Above 0, at a positive `intensity`, the instance is `continuous` (rendered on every capped frame) on purpose; at intensity 0 the output is the source and nothing renders. Off with reduced motion |
| `shader` | `water.wgsl` | WGSL text that replaces the shader (same bindings and uniform layout) |

`mountUI` adds the intensity slider.

## Actions

`handle.trigger(action, options)` ([semantics](../../docs/guides/EFFECTS-PLUGINS.md#5-actions-trigger-and-settled)).

| action | options | | resolves with |
|---|---|---|---|
| `drop` | `point`: `{x, y}` in canvas CSS px (default: the centre); `strength`: 0.08 to 2 (default 1.2, a tap's pebble); `swell`: `true` for a swell instead of a pebble | a pebble falls there at once, its depth in proportion to `strength`. A swell is one broad ring on the wake field (a crater of radius 24 CSS px with a crown, 10 CSS px deep at 1.2) that the wave equation carries outwards as one piece: a large, slow, reproducible displacement of the page | `'calm'`, once the surface is still again (about 3 s at the default intensity; a swell, about 2 s) |
| `demo` | none | a short rain: a first, larger raindrop near the centre, then a shower of 30 smaller ones that thins out over 1.4 s, then calm | `'calm'`, once the surface is still (about 3.5 s at the default intensity); `null` when the user takes over during the rain (the pointer, `setOptions`: the rain stops at once) |

`handle.settled()` resolves with `'calm'` once no ripple runs (pointer ripples count too; the ambient swell does not). A `point` that is not `{x, y}` or a non-finite `strength` rejects with a `TypeError`; a newer trigger resolves a pending one with `null` (and stops the rain of a demo). The plugin never starts the demo by itself: the page decides when to show it.

## Input

The plugin is `spatial`. `mapWaterPoint(state, point)` is the JavaScript twin of the displacement in `water.wgsl`: the same B-spline over the same 4 x 4 cells of the field, the same refraction, the same ambient waves, the same clamp. It evaluates the uniform block and the height field that were submitted for the presented frame (copied into one of four slots), so it describes exactly those pixels: clicks and typing reach the field the user sees, also while ripples run (0 of 150,000 pixels disagree in the GPU test). A gesture keeps one frozen mapping. The mapper clamps to the canvas.

**Touch.** The water covers the whole canvas and never claims a gesture: its listeners are passive and never call `preventDefault()`, so a finger keeps scrolling the page and the form keeps its own interaction. A tap makes a drop. A moving finger makes a wake, also while it scrolls the page: the browser then cancels the pointer (`pointercancel`), but its passive `touchmove` events go on, and each touch point is converted to canvas CSS px with the canvas where it is at that moment (it moves with the page), so the finger keeps stirring the water under it.

## Reduced motion

With `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` (read when the plugin is added, and by the host at every `trigger`), the water is still: the surface keeps its reflection and its tint, so it still shows what it is; no ripple starts (not from the pointer, not from `drop` or `demo`, which resolve `'calm'` at once), the ambient swell is off, and nothing animates, so the effect is rendered only when the page or an option changes. The mapper is then the identity.

## Performance

- **Zero work at rest.** At rest, and after every drop, wake and demo once the surface is still, the host renders no effect frame (0 in 3 s, measured in the gallery in headless Chrome and on the iPad below). A source change at rest renders one frame, which skips the field. The plugin requests frames only while the surface moves.
- **CPU.** The field keeps the box of cells that may be non-zero (every cell outside it is exactly 0), and the step and the check for a still surface run only over it and one cell around it, with bit-identical results. One step of the full 65,536-cell grid of a 1100 x 640 canvas costs about 0.35 ms (Node 24, Apple M1 Max); one pebble from rest costs 0.006 ms a step in its first 10 steps, 0.04 ms up to 0.5 s and 0.26 ms from 1 s on, when its rings cover most of the canvas. At 120 steps per second a stirred canvas is about 4.5 % of one core while it moves; the field (about 260 KB of r32float) is uploaded once per rendered frame then, never at rest.
- **GPU.** The caustics pass: a mesh of the surface, about 430,000 vertices at 1100 x 620 CSS px (358 strips of 2 x 599; 16 texel loads of the field each) and their triangles, 4x multisampled. It runs only while the surface moves, and draws only the part of the mesh that can differ from still water: the cells that may be non-zero, the reach of the B-spline around them, the farthest their light can land (the depth times the tilt of a ray refracted by the steepest slope, which the largest difference between neighbouring cells bounds) and a rim of flat surface. Elsewhere the light is that of still water, exactly 1, and the picture does not read it. The picture: per device pixel, 16 texel loads of the field and the B-spline (skipped while the field is flat, and for every pixel whose 4 x 4 cells lie outside the box), four samples of the light (only inside the region the pass drew) and one of the page. Neither depends on the number of taps; both shrink with the area that moves.
  - *How it is measured.* A bench renders the plugin alone, on its own device, at the gallery's canvas size (DPR 2), over a surface stirred by one tap in the middle or by 12 taps spread over the canvas. *Throughput*: 240 frames of one instant submitted back to back, the mean per frame (the whole frame: both passes), 3 runs. *Per pass*: timestamp queries on both passes, the median of 100 frames. *Cold*: one frame after 3 s of an idle GPU, 3 runs. The `timestampWrites('water')` hook of the host marks only the final pass, so the bench times the caustics pass itself.
  - *On the iPad* (2196 x 1276 device px, 2026-11-06), 0.1.0-preview.26 → now. One tap, 25 ms after it: 1.51 → 0.99 ms a frame (the caustics 0.55 → 0.09 ms, the picture 1.15 → 0.86 ms; cold, the caustics 1.71 → 0.27 ms). 250 ms after it: 1.63 → 1.17 ms; 500 ms: 1.87 → 1.51 ms. 12 taps, the whole canvas moving: 2.74 → 2.77 ms (the caustics 0.67 ms, the picture 2.3 ms), 7.6 to 9.7 ms cold. Still water (no caustics pass): 0.78 ms, measured on 0.1.0-preview.25.
  - *On the Mac* (Chrome, 2072 x 1276 device px): one tap, 25 ms after it, 0.24 → 0.13 ms (the caustics 0.15 → 0.03 ms, the picture 0.20 → 0.08 ms); 500 ms, 0.25 → 0.16 ms; 12 taps, 0.27 ms either way. In the gallery itself, at 120 Hz while taps stir the whole canvas, the timestamps from the start of the caustics pass to the end of the picture read 1.3 ms at the median (about 515 frames, 2 runs; the caustics 0.46 ms, the picture 1.1 to 1.2 ms): the GPU clocks lower when it gets one frame every 8.3 ms than when the bench feeds it frames back to back.
  - *The frames.* Frames at fixed times compared with 0.1.0-preview.26 (1100 x 640, a pebble in a corner and one in the middle, 6 times from 17 ms to 2.4 s): with `light: 0` identical (0 pixels), on the iPad and on the Mac. With the caustics, outside the region the pass draws they are identical; inside it this GPU adds up the light of the caustic lines differently whenever the draw changes shape, even by triangles that light nothing (4 columns more beyond the edge): that alone changes 230 to 7,300 pixels of 704,000 by up to 42 levels on both machines, and the region changes 270 to 8,000 by up to 69 levels, in the same places. The GPU test holds both: bit-identical outside the region, inside it no more than that variance.
- **On the iPad.** iPad Pro 11-inch (3rd generation, M1), iPadOS 27.2, Safari, 120 Hz, DPR 2, the plugin gallery uncapped (2026-11-05, with the drops of the previous design; the pebbles cost less): 0 effect frames in 3 s at rest; the demo at 120 effect frames per second, intervals 8.34 ms at p50 and 8.54 ms at p95; a 0.7 s wake at 119 per second, p95 8.52 ms; 0 effect frames in 3 s after each. Taps (pointerdown and pointerup events of pointerType touch dispatched by a script in the page on the element under the point, not a physical finger; 10 a second for 3 s, or 30 in 1 s, the gallery's frame-cap slider made inert; 2026-11-05, preview.24): 118 effect frames per second, p95 8.54 ms. Without that, one of those taps lands on the slider, which sets `canvas.frameCap` to 55: the host then renders at the cap, about 60 frames per second, as it should. After a 4 s stir the water is at rest about 4.6 s later: the ripples still move the page by 0.4 px 2.5 s after the stir, and by less than 0.1 px from about 3.5 s on. Measured again on 2026-11-06 (taps dispatched in the page 8 a second for 4 s away from the form's controls, a fast drag for 4 s, rest): taps at 120 effect frames per second, p95 8.5 ms, the longest interval 9.6 to 12.7 ms; the drag at 119 per second, p95 8.5 ms, with two longer intervals (22 to 30 ms) where the press and the release reach the form, as before; JS of a frame 0.9 ms (the step 0.8 ms; the upload 0.06 ms), no typed array made per frame; 0 effect frames at rest. A tap from rest: the JS of its first frames 0.3 to 0.5 ms (1.5 to 1.7 ms in 0.1.0-preview.25), the first frame done 7 ms after its submission (10 to 12 ms); the first tap after the page loads 0.8 to 2.7 ms (4 to 6 ms). Its second frame still comes one frame late in about half the taps from rest (11 to 20 ms after the first), and this is the platform's, not the plugin's: measured again on 2026-11-06 (taps from rest 5.5 s apart, the first tap after each page load left out), the late share is 5 of 8 with 0.1.0-preview.26, 11 of 18 with the caustics pass over its region (the GPU work of the frame a third lower), 8 of 18 for a control plugin that only copies the page (its frame done 5 ms after submission), and 5 of 10 when the plugin also wakes the GPU with a small pass at the pointerdown itself (its first frame done 7 ms after submission instead of 9). On a touch screen nothing arrives earlier than the pointerdown (no hover; `pointerover` and `touchstart` come with it), so the plugin cannot start sooner, and it keeps no loop at rest to keep the GPU awake.
- **On the Mac.** Chrome, headed, 120 Hz (MacBook Pro, Liquid Retina XDR), trusted clicks on the water away from the form's controls, 10 a second for 3 s and 30 in 1 s: 120 effect frames per second, the longest frame interval 9.4 ms, no main-thread task over 8.33 ms (the longest 6.3 ms), the first frames after a click from rest 7.3 to 9.4 ms apart. Again on 2026-11-06 with taps dispatched by a script in the page (8 a second for 4 s) and a fast drag: 120 effect frames per second, p95 8.5 to 9.3 ms, the longest interval 9.3 ms; JS of a frame 0.6 to 0.8 ms, GPU 1.3 ms for the caustics and the picture (timestamp queries on both passes, see GPU above); about 1 KB of short-lived objects a frame (the descriptors of the passes), no typed array. The first tap after the page loads no longer makes a main-thread task over 8.33 ms (before: one of 10 to 11 ms, the first steps of the full grid in cold code; now the longest is 3.4 to 3.9 ms), its first frame's JS 0.8 to 1 ms instead of 4.6 to 7.1 ms.

## Limitations

- The wakes run on the CPU (so that the input mapper reads exactly what the GPU draws), on a grid of cells of at least 3 CSS px: ripples shorter than about 4 cells are smoothed away by the B-spline. The wave equation has no dispersion: the rings of a pebble come from its train of impulses.
- The caustics come from one light, from straight above, onto the page as a flat floor 320 CSS px below the surface (the page casts no shadow and has no depth of its own). Over dark content the caustic light shows mostly through the light the water scatters along its lines; over light content (buttons, text) through the page itself.
- The pebbles are linear water waves: no splash, no crown of droplets, no jet above the surface. Every pebble has the same shape and differs only in depth.
- The environment that the water reflects is fixed (a sky, a window light up and to the left, a sun up and to the right); it is not the page's surroundings.
- A resize to another grid starts with a flat surface (a resize by a fraction of a cell keeps the ripples).
- WebGPU only (without an adapter `createHost` rejects with a `NotSupportedError`). Runs over the polyfill and over a browser's own HTML-in-Canvas implementation ([what differs there](../../docs/guides/EFFECTS-PLUGINS.md#on-a-native-implementation)); the native suite (`npm run test:native-effects`) runs it.
